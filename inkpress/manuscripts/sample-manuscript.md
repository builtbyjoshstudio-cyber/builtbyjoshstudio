## The First Round

The lamps went on in order---east side first, then west---because that was
how the road curved, and because Mara had walked it that way for eleven years.

She didn't count them anymore. Counting was for the first year, when the round
still felt like a thing you could get wrong. Now it was just the shape of the
evening: forty-one lamps, one long street, the harbour going dark behind her.

"You're early," the harbourmaster said.

"I'm never early. You're just late noticing."

* * *

By the time she reached the twelfth lamp, the fog had come in off the water and
turned every flame into a soft, uncertain coin. She liked it this way. On clear
nights the street looked finished. In fog it looked like something still being
decided.

> A lamp is not light. A lamp is a promise that someone came by.

Her grandmother had said that, or something close to it, in the '90s when the
gas lines still ran under Harbour Row and the whole trade was a different job.

### What the Round Costs

Eleven years is long enough to learn the weight of a thing. The pole, the wick,
the particular ache that arrives in the left shoulder somewhere around lamp
thirty and stays until morning.

It is also long enough to stop mentioning it.

## The Second Round

She turned back at the seawall and started down the other side, and the lamps
she had lit an hour ago were still burning, which was the entire point, and
which never once stopped being a small surprise.

The fog thinned. The road came back. Somewhere behind her a window went yellow,
then another---people waking into the evening, the way they do.

Mara shifted the pole to her right shoulder and kept walking.
